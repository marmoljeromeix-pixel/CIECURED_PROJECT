In the classroom

What would you do if a classmate touched you without your consent?

During a classroom activity, a male student repeatedly touches a female classmate’s body parts even though she is uncomfortable with it. She moves away, but he does it again and says he is only joking. The student feels uneasy and is unsure whether she should report it.

The student should move away and seek help from someone she trusts, such as her teacher or a GAD representative. She can share what happened and report the behavior if she feels comfortable doing so. Through GAD, she can receive support and guidance while ensuring that her concern is handled respectfully and confidentially.

Consider

Move to a safer space if you're able to. What you do next is your call.

Reach out

A trusted friend, adviser, or counselor can help you think it through.

Reporting

Within the faculty

What would you do if a professor crossed a personal boundary?

A position of authority should never be used to pressure or intimidate a student.

Unwanted comments, messages, or pressure from someone with authority over you — a professor, adviser, or supervisor — is a misuse of that authority. It isn't something you caused.

Consider

Save messages or details if it's safe to. Power gaps make this hard alone, and that's okay.

Reach out

Confidential support is available, even if you're unsure it "counts."

Reporting

Campus channels exist specifically for cases involving faculty or staff.

Safety first

You're never responsible for how someone in authority behaves.

Are You Aware of Your Rights?

CIEcured translates the protections you're entitled to into plain language — so you can recognize what's happening and know exactly what to do next.

RA 9262

Anti-VAWC Law

Hover to learn more ›

Protects women and their children from physical, sexual, psychological, and economic abuse — including by a partner or family member.

Applies to a spouse, ex-spouse, or partner — even without marriage or shared residence. Covers threats, controlling behavior, and withholding financial support, not just physical harm.

RA 11313

Safe Spaces Act

Hover to learn more ›

Covers harassment in streets, public spaces, online, and educational or workplace settings — gender-based, in any form.

Includes catcalling, stalking, unwanted advances, and online harassment such as unwanted sexual remarks, threats, or sharing private content without consent.

RA 7877

Anti-Sexual Harassment Act

Hover to learn more ›

Addresses harassment by someone who holds authority, influence, or moral ascendancy over you at school or at work.

Covers a supervisor, teacher, or someone in a position of power who conditions a favor, grade, or job outcome on sexual demands — even implied ones.

Online campus life

What would you do if your class group chat became a place for harassment?

Harassment can happen even when you're behind a screen.

Unwanted messages, shared photos, or rumors in a class or org chat are still harassment — the fact that it happened online doesn't make it less real.

Consider

Save screenshots if you can. Blocking or muting is a valid response.

Reach out

Loop in a trusted classmate, adviser, or the group's moderator.

Reporting

Report it if it's tied to your class, org, or campus community.

Safety first

You're not obligated to respond to or engage with the sender.

Student life

What would you do if someone pressured you into something you didn't want?

You never owe someone something because they're your friend, partner, classmate, or org member.

Pressure, guilt-tripping, or "everyone's doing it" can make real consent hard to give freely. You can change your mind at any point, even after saying yes.

Consider

It's okay to pause, say no, or step away — whatever the setting.

Reach out

Talk to someone you trust, even if you're unsure it "counts" as anything serious.

Reporting

As a bystander

What would you do if you witnessed someone being harassed on campus?

You don't have to be the person experiencing harassment to help create a safer campus.

Noticing something is often the first step. You don't need to be certain, and you don't have to handle it alone — small actions can still make a real difference.

Consider

A quiet "are you okay?" can matter more than confronting anyone.

Reach out

Help them reach someone they trust, or just stay nearby.

Reporting
